Long Island Expressway

Traffic speed analytics from public NYSDOT cameras (student prototype)

Map Speed estimates Calibrate a camera About and ethics

What this is

A student project from ProjectQuack (Stony Brook). It measures how fast traffic moves past a few public traffic cameras on the Long Island Expressway (I-495) and shows the results as traffic statistics: typical speeds, the spread of speeds, and how many vehicles appear to exceed the posted limit.

What this is not

It is not speed enforcement and its numbers are not evidence that anyone broke the law. In New York, automated speed enforcement exists only where state law creates it, such as New York City school-zone cameras (Vehicle and Traffic Law §1180-b) and highway work-zone cameras run by NYSDOT and the Thruway Authority (§1180-e). Those programs use certified equipment, sworn technician certificates and government operators. This project has none of that.

Privacy

  • Vehicles are anonymous tracks: a number that exists only inside one processed clip, a vehicle class (car, truck, bus, motorcycle), a time, a camera, and a speed estimate with its uncertainty.
  • No licence-plate reading, no face detection, and no attempt to identify a vehicle, a driver, or to follow a vehicle from one camera to the next. The camera resolution (about 640×480) could not support that anyway, and we will not add it.
  • No images of individual vehicles are stored or shown. The only image kept per camera is a calibration frame of the empty road, made by taking the median of many frames so that moving vehicles disappear.
  • Retention: short research clips are deleted after processing; per-frame track positions are kept for at most 7 days for debugging; only per-vehicle speed estimates and aggregates are kept longer.

How a speed is estimated

A detector (YOLO) finds vehicles in each video frame and a tracker (ByteTrack) links them from frame to frame. For every camera we calibrate a mapping from image pixels to metres on the road, using the standard lane markings: dashed lines are 10 ft long with 30 ft gaps, and lanes are 12 ft wide. The bottom-centre of each vehicle's box is mapped onto the road, and a robust straight-line fit of position against the stream's frame timestamps gives the speed.

Every estimate comes with a 1-sigma uncertainty that combines the scatter of the fit with the calibration's scale uncertainty (about 2.5%, mostly paint tolerance). A vehicle is flagged only when its estimate minus a margin (the larger of 2 sigma and 3 mph) is still above the posted limit. Tracks that are too short, too jumpy, or not moving along the road are rejected rather than guessed.

Known limits

  • Most LIE cameras are pan-tilt-zoom. When an operator moves one, its calibration is wrong; we compare each clip with the calibration frame and reject speeds if the view changed.
  • Night, rain, sun glare and heavy congestion (vehicles hiding each other) reduce accuracy.
  • NYC DOT cameras in Queens publish still images about every 2 seconds. That is too slow to follow a vehicle, so they are not used for speeds.
  • The method has not yet been validated against ground truth. The plan is a test drive past a camera at a steady, GPS-logged speed, and comparison with NYC DOT's published travel speeds in Queens.
